# Casino Steakhouse Beverage Profitability Program

A sanitized business case connecting P&L analysis to menu strategy, server certification, host training, and manager controls.

## The problem

An upscale steakhouse inside a casino property had a profitability gap. Adding fixed capacity was not the immediate answer. I needed to determine whether the existing guest volume, product mix, and service routines contained an opportunity the floor team could act on.

I wrote the case in July 2024 using June operating statements. At the time, I was an Operations Manager who was later promoted to Assistant General Manager and Acting General Manager under a contract engagement.

## The reasoning

I started by separating food and beverage economics, then compared category margins, beverage share of revenue, average guest spend, and menu-basket potential. That analysis pointed to beverage attachment as the most practical lever to test.

The model followed five questions:

1. Which categories contributed the strongest margin?
2. How much of current revenue came from beverage?
3. Where did actual guest spend differ from a reasonable menu basket?
4. What would one additional glass of wine per guest contribute under stated assumptions?
5. How did casino comps affect the view of cash revenue and service cost?

The objective was not a larger spreadsheet. It was a chain from financial signal to behavior the team could learn, practice, and measure.

## The models

Category margin:

```text
category margin = category revenue minus category cost divided by category revenue
```

Revenue-weighted beverage margin:

```text
beverage margin = total beverage revenue minus total beverage cost divided by total beverage revenue
```

Incremental wine scenario:

```text
incremental revenue = monthly covers multiplied by average glass price
incremental cost = incremental revenue multiplied by pour cost ratio
marginal profit = incremental revenue minus incremental cost
```

An illustrative scenario using 3,000 covers, a $15 glass, and a 20 percent pour cost produces $45,000 in incremental revenue and $36,000 in marginal profit per month. It is a sensitivity example, not an achieved result.

The comp-adjusted model tested cash activity separately from comped food revenue under explicit proportional-cost assumptions. It was an analytical lens rather than an audited view of profitability.

## From the model to the floor

I translated the opportunity into three connected operating programs.

The proposed wine menu paired plain-language taste descriptions with specific dishes, price tiers, and a concise reason a server could use at the table.

The six-part server program combined product education, sales language, written checks, role-play, final assessment, and manager reinforcement.

The four-week host program addressed greetings, reservations, wait management, service recovery, table rotation, head-count balancing, cross-team communication, observation, and sign-off.

The common mechanism was simple: define the standard, rehearse it, assess it, and reinforce it.

## What the repository establishes

This case documents the analytical method, wine-menu proposal, server certification structure, and host training design. It demonstrates how I connected operating data to a program the floor team could execute.

It does not claim that property leadership approved the full proposal, that every component was implemented, or that the illustrative financial upside was realized. The working benchmark also remains a planning assumption unless a supporting source is added.
